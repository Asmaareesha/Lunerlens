How the model works

Sun and Earth directions use the mean lunar cycle (29.53 days), the Moon’s axial tilt (about 1.5°) and approximate libration. Terrain is not modelled: a small offset stands in for high ground at the polar and highland sites. Ice scores are rough, qualitative estimates. Treat results as a teaching model, not mission planning.

About this project

Choosing where to land on the Moon is an engineering decision as much as a scientific one. A site needs sunlight for power, a line of sight to Earth for communication, and ideally water ice nearby. No site wins on all three, so every mission is a tradeoff. LunarLens lets you see that tradeoff for yourself and change the priorities.

I built it as an independent project on the problem behind NASA’s Space Apps challenge on comparing lunar landing sites, sunlight, and communication windows. It is not affiliated with NASA. I am interested in physics, aviation, and aerospace, and I wanted to turn those interests into something people can use.

What it teaches: the Sun’s angle changes over a 29.5-day lunar cycle, so most places on the Moon have about two weeks of night. Libration lets Earth drift in and out of view at the edges of the near side. The far side never sees Earth, so it needs a relay satellite. The poles trade long daylight for a grazing, fragile link to Earth.

What I would improve next: use real ephemeris data in place of my approximations, add terrain and illumination maps, and test the scoring against published mission analyses.

Sites checked against NASA, LROC, and mission sources: Malapert (86°S, 0°E) and the Chang’e-4 landing site (45.46°S, 177.59°E). Ice scores are rough qualitative estimates, not measurements.
